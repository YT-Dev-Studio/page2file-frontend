Automatic segmentation is a draft, not editorial judgment. Review the order, split overloaded sections and merge fragments that make sense only together.

## Find the real section boundaries

A semantic section often begins with a heading and ends before the next topic. Design changes can reinforce that boundary, but a background color alone does not always mean a new slide. Navigation, cookie notices and repeated calls to action are usually interface material rather than presentation content.

Keep a chart with its title and legend, an image with its caption, and a code example with the explanation that makes it useful.

## Split tall sections without shrinking them

A 16:9 slide cannot hold the same vertical density as a webpage. Continue a long list, table or article section across slides and repeat the minimum context a reader needs. Do not stretch screenshots or reduce body text until it cannot be presented.

For screenshot sections, crop along meaningful boundaries. For editable sections, redistribute objects while preserving reading order.

## Choose one output policy

Screenshot slides preserve composition and make quick design references. Editable slides support revision but expose format substitutions and raster fallbacks. The [mode comparison](/en/blog/screenshot-vs-editable-powerpoint) gives concrete review points.

If the source is a single URL, [One Page 2 PowerPoint](/en/one-page2powerpoint-gpt) keeps the boundary simple. For several pages, [Web 2 PowerPoint](/en/web2powerpoint-gpt) returns separate decks. When the source is rendered HTML, first review the [HTML-to-PowerPoint boundary](/en/blog/html-to-powerpoint).

## Run a slide-by-slide check

Read titles in sequence without the body: they should tell a coherent story. Then check image sharpness, source links, font substitutions, overlaps and elements outside the slide canvas.

Delete a weak slide rather than keeping it to match a page count. A deck is successful when a reader can follow it, not when every webpage box has been copied.

## Distinguish structural and visual boundaries

HTML headings provide an outline, but screenshot sections do not always follow the heading tree. A hero may contain a heading, illustration and call to action that belong on one slide. A comparison may use several columns under one heading and need multiple slides to stay readable.

Map both structures before conversion. Record the semantic hierarchy for order and the visual groups for composition. When they disagree, choose the boundary that preserves meaning and keep enough context to show where the material came from.

Cards require judgment. A row of four short cards may work as one slide, while four dense product descriptions need separate slides or a summary. Avoid a deck whose only rule is “one browser block per slide.”

## Control density and repetition

Projected slides need larger text and fewer simultaneous ideas than most webpages. Use a consistent density target rather than scaling each section independently. If one slide has readable text and the next uses tiny labels, the deck will feel unstable even when both fit.

Sticky navigation, cookie banners and repeated page headers should not consume slide space unless they provide required attribution. Remove duplication during preparation or review. When a repeated table heading helps comprehension, keep it deliberately.

Section backgrounds can provide continuity, but a complex full-page background may make editable text hard to read. Preserve it as a visual layer when it carries design meaning, or simplify it while retaining attribution.

## Preserve evidence and editing intent

When a chart or diagram becomes an image, keep its title, legend and source together. Cropping decorative margins is acceptable; cropping an axis label changes the evidence. Do not describe a screenshot fallback as an editable chart.

For editable slides, test ordinary editing tasks. Change a title, adjust a text box and replace one image. Confirm that object order remains correct and that grouped elements do not unexpectedly separate.

## Write a source-to-slide map

List the approved webpage sections in order, then assign each to a slide, a range of slides or an explicit exclusion. This small map exposes two common failures: content that disappeared during segmentation and interface fragments that became slides without an editorial purpose.

Use the map to preserve context when one source section becomes several slides. Repeat a short section label, table heading or chart title where the audience would otherwise lose orientation. Do not repeat whole navigation bars to achieve the same effect.

Record source links or identifiers for claims that require traceability. If several slides come from one long section, they can share one source note as long as the relationship remains clear.

## Handle difficult components

A wide comparison can become several slides that keep the same criteria while showing fewer columns. A long table can continue with repeated headers and a clear continuation label. A timeline should preserve sequence; splitting it into unrelated crops can change the story.

Charts need their title, axes, units and legend. If those elements cannot remain readable as separate objects, preserve the complete chart visually and place editable interpretation beside or after it. Never rebuild exact values from a screenshot when accuracy matters.

Accordions, tabs and carousels require a selected state. Open only the panels that belong in the deck and explain their order. Hidden alternatives are not part of the rendered capture, and placing every possible state in one deck may create repetition rather than completeness.

## Check pacing at presentation size

Run the deck in slideshow mode and estimate what the audience can understand before the next slide. A slide with several browser paragraphs may be technically legible at a desk and still be impossible to present. Split it around ideas, not arbitrary height.

Watch scale across consecutive slides. A tiny screenshot followed by oversized editable text makes the deck feel inconsistent and can hide which details matter. Set a readable floor and change the section plan when material cannot meet it.

Check the opening and ending separately. The first slide should establish the source and subject; the final slide should close the intended narrative. A webpage hero and footer are not automatically suitable presentation bookends.

## Finish with an editable-or-visual declaration

For each slide, know whether recipients can revise its important content. Editable text may coexist with visual charts or grouped fallbacks. Include that distinction in handoff notes when later editing is part of the job.
